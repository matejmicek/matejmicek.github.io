T/The thesis · 60 seconds

The most contested real estate in agent infra.
Incumbents keep the revenue, concede the argument.

In May this looked like a promising picks-and-shovels category with a few clear leaders. Twelve weeks later it is a knife fight: the leaders re-rated violently, the big clouds all arrived at once, the AI labs started buying companies in the layer instead of just renting from them, and the industry is having a genuine argument about whether the founding product idea — a disposable "sandbox" — is even the right one. One pattern runs through everything below: the companies making the money keep adopting the language of their critics while giving up none of the revenue. Watching whose vocabulary wins tells you more about the next twelve months than the funding announcements do.

Where value is accruing.
Modal reached ~$300M in annual revenue with sandboxes now over a third of it and growing 40–50% every month. Prime Intellect went from zero to $100M revenue and a $1B valuation in under a year selling RL training infrastructure. Daytona is growing 74% month-over-month. E2B quietly became the default sandbox under dozens of successful agent products. And Cisco paying ~$300M for Astrix proved agent-security wedges have real exits.
Where it isn't.
The basic sandbox technology itself has become a commodity — Tencent and Alibaba now give away free, API-compatible clones of the market leader. Computer-use startups have lots of mindshare but almost no funding, and two of the five best-known ones already gave up and pivoted. WebAssembly-based sandboxing quietly died as a thesis (its flagship company was absorbed into Akamai). And the first casualties are in: Riza shut down, Morph looks abandoned, Val Town's revenue shrank in June.
Three questions worth a call this week.
1 · Browserbase claims its usage grew ~8x but hasn't raised in 14 months — discipline, or growth that isn't converting to revenue? Its CEO said on tape he's working on a Series C. 2 · Do the RL-environment startups have real recurring revenue, or does the whole category depend on five or six AI labs' training budgets? 3 · Anthropic and Cognition now let sandbox vendors compete as interchangeable "approved providers" under their products — is winning such a slot real distribution, or the first step toward being commoditized?
P/The 3-minute primer · read this and the rest of the map needs no lookups

How this market actually works.

Five ideas explain almost everything in this map. If you know them already, skip to the deltas.

  • 1 · Why agents need sandboxes at all. An AI agent doing real work has to execute code it wrote itself, click around websites, and install software — all actions you would never allow on a machine holding your data, because the agent can be wrong or manipulated. So every agent product gives its agent a disposable, walled-off computer in the cloud: a "sandbox." When the task ends, the computer is thrown away. The business opportunity is that agents consume computers at absurd rates — one Daytona customer alone spins up ~850,000 per day — and someone has to sell them.
  • 2 · The isolation technologies, in one paragraph. The walls of the sandbox can be built four ways, trading safety against speed. Containers (classic Docker) share the host machine's operating-system kernel — fast, but a determined attacker who breaks the kernel breaks into everything, which is why the industry considers them insufficient for hostile code. MicroVMs — tiny virtual machines, each with its own kernel — are the market's default answer; the standard implementation is Firecracker, which Amazon built for AWS Lambda and open-sourced (boots a full machine in ~125 milliseconds). gVisor, from Google, is a middle path: a fake kernel in software that intercepts everything the sandboxed program asks the real kernel to do — slightly weaker walls than a microVM, much easier to run at massive scale, which is why Modal and Google use it. And V8 isolates — the sandboxing built into Chrome's JavaScript engine — start in microseconds but have the thinnest walls; Cloudflare's whole edge network runs on them, so Cloudflare is betting they're enough. There is no agreed winner, and that disagreement is itself one of the twelve conflicts at the bottom of this page.
  • 3 · Why "cold start" and "forking" are the numbers everyone brags about. Cold start is how long a fresh computer takes to be ready — it matters because an agent that pauses three seconds per step feels broken, so vendors compete from ~250ms down toward ~10ms. Forking (also: snapshotting) means freezing a warm computer's entire memory and disk, then cloning it instantly — so a hundred copies of the same prepared machine can explore a problem in parallel. Forking is the technology behind both parallel coding agents and the RL-training workloads below, and a whole micro-industry of "fork a warm VM in under a millisecond" projects appeared this year.
  • 4 · What "RL environments" are and why money is flooding in. The frontier labs improve their models with reinforcement learning: put the model in a realistic situation, let it try the task thousands of times, score every attempt, reward what works. That requires realistic situations — a fake Salesforce, a fake Excel, a fake bank back-office, each with tasks and an automatic grader. Companies that build these simulated workplaces ("RL environments") sell them to labs for millions per contract, because the labs' constraint has shifted from raw data to realistic practice-worlds. It's the fastest-growing new category in this map — and the most concentrated, because there are only five or six labs to sell to. Sandboxes and RL environments are converging into one market: every training run needs thousands of disposable computers to host the simulated worlds.
  • 5 · Four acronyms that carry half the arguments below. BYOC ("bring your own cloud") means the vendor's software runs inside the customer's own AWS/Google account instead of the vendor's — banks and pharma demand this for compliance, and it's becoming the enterprise battleground. An egress proxy is a checkpoint on the sandbox's outbound internet traffic; the modern version also injects passwords and API keys at the proxy so the agent itself never sees a secret it could leak — this "credential brokering" idea is the year's most important new security concept. MCP is the now-standard protocol for connecting agents to tools. And a license-and-hire deal (Google's move on Mechanize, copied from the Windsurf playbook) means the acquirer licenses the technology non-exclusively and hires the founders — the founders get paid, but investors don't get a real acquisition multiple. It matters because it's becoming the default way labs "buy" hot startups.
Δ/What actually changed · Feb–Aug 2026

The six changes that matter.

Each one written as: what happened, why it happened, and what it means if you're deciding where to invest. Everything is sourced and dated; nothing here was true in the May map.

  • Δ1 · Every big cloud entered the market at once — the "giants won't bother" thesis is dead. The May map assumed hyperscalers would leave agent sandboxes to startups. Between March and August, six platform giants shipped competing products: Cloudflare launched sandboxes, then an agent runtime, then its own browser built from scratch for agents (Kitesurf). Google made its Kubernetes-based Agent Sandbox generally available — usage grew 16x in five months, with LangChain and Lovable as customers. AWS shipped two separate products, and pointedly, the first showcase for its new Lambda MicroVMs was running Anthropic's self-hosted sandboxes. CoreWeave and Docker followed. Why it matters: every enterprise buyer now has a "just use what's already in our cloud" option, which is usually the default-safe choice. The startups' remaining edges are real but narrower — working across all clouds, moving faster, and being the neutral choice when the customer doesn't want deeper hyperscaler lock-in. One caveat cuts the other way: in August, AWS, Google and Vercel all disclosed the same class of security bug in their agent stacks ("CoreBreak") — being a giant does not yet mean being better at this.
  • Δ2 · RL environments went from footnote to a billion-dollar category in six months. This category wasn't on the May map at all; today three independent trackers count 35–60 startups building simulated software worlds for model training (see primer #4). The money is remarkable: Prime Intellect raised $130M at a $1B valuation with a claimed $100M of revenue and 6,000 customers; Applied Compute went from launch to a $1.3B valuation in under ten months; Mechanize raised a $9.1M seed at a $500M valuation and 103 days later Google was reportedly negotiating a $1.5B+ license-and-hire deal for it. Demand is spilling into the sandbox layer too — Daytona says RL and evaluation workloads went from 0% to about half of everything running on its infrastructure. The bear case is structural: most of these companies sell to the same five or six labs, and the Mechanize-style exit pays founders without paying the cap table. The Deeptune story is the cautionary memo — it raised $43M from a16z in March and was acquired by Mercor four months later, by an acquirer whose CEO had angel-invested in that very round "with acquisition in mind" (his words to Fortune).
  • Δ3 · The word "sandbox" lost the argument — while sandboxes kept all the money. A real intellectual fight broke out over the category's founding idea. One side says disposable, throwaway sandboxes are the wrong abstraction: real work accumulates state — databases, logins, half-finished projects — so agents need a persistent computer they own. Martin Casado of a16z made this explicit when backing a new startup called Runta ("AI agents just want a computer… this isn't yet another sandbox cloud"), and Fly.io staked its relaunch on it ("The age of sandboxes is over"). The other side simply points at its metrics: Modal raised $355M while Fly raised $25M, one Daytona customer burns through 850,000 disposable sandboxes a day, and training workloads are inherently throwaway. Watch what the incumbents did, though: E2B's homepage now says "computers for agents," Daytona sells "composable computers," and everyone shipped persistence features (pause/resume, 24-hour sessions, snapshots). They conceded the framing, absorbed the features, and kept the revenue — which is usually how these arguments end.
  • Δ4 · Open source flipped from moat to battlefield. In May, being open source was treated as the trust moat in this market. Then on June 11 Daytona — the second-biggest pure-play — closed its source code entirely, arguing that publishing your isolation layer hands "the exact blueprint" to AI-powered vulnerability hunters that "never get tired." Hacker News rejected the reasoning as security-by-obscurity, and Daytona's GitHub momentum actually went negative. One month later its rival E2B declared itself "the only major open-source agentic sandbox platform" and paired that with EU-AI-Act-compliance marketing in the same month the Act's obligations took effect — weaponizing a licensing decision it had made back in 2023. Meanwhile the open flank got attacked from below: Tencent released a free clone that is API-compatible with E2B ("switch by changing one env var," 11K GitHub stars in four months), and an Alibaba-linked equivalent followed. The refined lesson for diligence: open source in this market is now a distribution and standards play (E2B's API becoming the compatibility target is worth more than its star count), not a revenue signal — the two best-monetizing vendors, Modal and Daytona, have the least open code.
  • Δ5 · The biggest customers keep leaving the market — labs now build or buy their own execution layer. A pattern the May map missed entirely: every player operating at frontier scale concluded that renting sandboxes doesn't work at their size. Perplexity built its own sandbox platform in ten weeks. Anthropic runs Claude's code execution in its own gVisor containers. OpenAI went furthest — it acquired Ona (formerly Gitpod) in June to give Codex persistent multi-day sandboxes, after also buying Astral and hiring OpenClaw's creator. Cognition built a custom hypervisor that cut VM snapshot times from 30 minutes to seconds. Replit runs its own stack. The twist: these same companies then became the sandbox vendors' biggest distribution channels, running "approved provider" programs (Claude Managed Agents, Devin Outposts, Stripe Projects) where vendors compete for slots underneath them. The investment takeaway: the vendor market serves the long tail of thousands of agent companies — a real and growing business — but its ceiling is capped, because the customers big enough to define the category are precisely the ones that graduate off it.
  • Δ6 · Security became the main storyline — and the fear is selling the wrong product. The year's security drama was OpenClaw: a viral self-hosted personal agent (a quarter-million GitHub stars) that normal people ran on exposed servers — 135,000 internet-reachable installations, 138+ security flaws catalogued by August, and the first CVE ever issued against an agentic system. That panic drove real spending all year. But here's the nuance that matters: every headline "sandbox escape" of 2026 — Kimi K3, an OpenAI incident, a Claude Code bug — turned out on inspection to be a configuration failure (the sandbox allowed something it shouldn't have), not a failure of the isolation technology itself; the UK's AI Security Institute tested escape attempts systematically and found zero novel exploits. The recurring real bug class is authorization — systems trusting that because a tool call arrived, the model must have approved it (that's the CoreBreak bug that hit AWS, Google and Vercel simultaneously). So the products that actually fix the problem are egress proxies and credential brokers (primer #5): Infisical's Agent Vault got the warmest security reception on HN this year, and Cisco paid ~$250–350M for Astrix. If you buy one thing from this delta: the money is in the authorization layer, not in ever-thicker virtual-machine walls.

DaytonaEU
Then

A Croatian-founded, open-source developer-environments company that had pivoted into agent sandboxes; the May map knew it as an $11M-seed business absorbing the users of a dead competitor (Riza).

Now

The most eventful six months of any vendor. It raised a $24M Series A from FirstMark in February with Datadog and Figma as strategic investors, relaunched its entire product (the CEO openly told V1 customers "we'll help you move to competitors"), and shipped faster than anyone: GPU sandboxes, Windows sandboxes (with Microsoft licensing handled — a real rarity), VM forking, a secrets manager, a network firewall. Daytona traded away the exact open-source trust moat the May map credited it with, betting that its real future customers — RL and enterprise workloads — prefer a managed, closed product anyway. The Windows offering is the wedge to watch: enterprise back-office automation lives on Windows, and almost nobody else can sell it at scale.

Modal
Then

Known primarily as a serverless GPU/inference platform; sandboxes were a side product. Valued at $1.1B as of September 2025.

Now

The category's biggest re-rating: a $355M Series C at $4.65B in May, on ~$300M of annualised revenue (5x in eight months). Sandboxes are the story — over a third of revenue, growing 40–50% per month, a billion launched cumulatively, and an engineering post about running one million sandboxes concurrently that won over even the skeptics on Hacker News. It acquired Butter (a computer-use team that had built its own micro-kernel), renamed its developer-experience function to "Agent Experience," and its CEO says openly that it is shopping for more acquisitions. New startups now build on Modal by default the way earlier startups built on AWS.

So what

Modal went from "GPU cloud with a sandbox feature" to the default execution substrate of the agent economy, colliding head-on with E2B and Daytona. It also carries the least concentration risk of the pure infrastructure players, because sandboxes are one of four product lines rather than the whole company.

Browserbase
Then

The category leader in managed browsers for agents — $40M Series B at a $300M valuation in June 2025, famous for the thesis that the "icky parts" of web automation (CAPTCHAs, proxies, logins) are a moat the AI labs will never want to own.

Now

Repositioned from browser infrastructure to a "browser agent platform": it launched a fully managed agent you call with one API (running on 35M+ browser sessions a month), rewrote its Stagehand automation framework to be 2x faster and ~80% cheaper in tokens than Playwright, and added search and data APIs. CEO Paul Klein has publicly mused about renaming the company. Two anomalies matter for an investor: despite claiming roughly 8x usage growth, it hasn't raised in 14 months — though Klein said on a podcast, "I'm out of relationships right now for my Series C… I gotta start all over again," so a raise is being worked. And when Anthropic picked a browser partner for Claude's managed agents, it chose the smaller Kernel, not Browserbase.

So what

Browserbase is being squeezed from three directions at once — Cloudflare built an agent browser from scratch above it, the open-source Browser Use undercuts it at $0.02 per browser-hour below it, and Kernel took the most prestigious partner slot sideways. The terms of its next round, whenever it lands, are the single best read on whether standalone browser infrastructure remains a venture-scale category.

Cloudflare · Vercel · Docker
Then

In May: Cloudflare appeared only as a footnote for Durable Objects, Vercel's sandbox was brand new, and Docker wasn't in the market at all.

Now

Cloudflare built a complete agent-computing stack in five months — sandboxes (GA in April, with Figma as launch customer), an agent runtime that automatically routes work between ultra-cheap isolates and full Linux containers, and Kitesurf, a browser engine written from scratch for agents rather than humans. Its engineers make the market's most radical claim: agents don't need containers at all ("we are gonna make it work on a Cloudflare Worker"). Vercel made its Firecracker-based sandbox generally available, then shipped eve, an open-source agent framework bundling durable execution — putting it in direct competition with the workflow startups (Inngest, Trigger.dev) it used to merely host. Docker relaunched Docker Sandboxes in August on a custom-built VM engine; it drew the year's biggest Hacker News discussion (693 points), much of it complaints about the login wall and the fact that Linux still isn't supported eight months in.

So what

None of the three compete on sandbox price — each bundles an adjacent layer (a browser, a framework, enterprise governance) into a platform its customers already pay for. The pressure on pure-play startups will arrive through bundling, not a price war.

AWS · Google · Azure · CoreWeave
Then

AWS's AgentCore existed as a managed agent service, but no hyperscaler sold a dedicated sandbox product.

Now

AWS now ships both sides of the industry's biggest argument as one product line: short-lived serverless microVMs and 14-day durable machines, plus a brand-new standalone product (Lambda MicroVMs) whose first showcase was running Anthropic's self-hosted sandboxes. Its VP said on the record: "I don't think anybody else has the level of security we do." Google made GKE Agent Sandbox generally available — 16x usage growth in five months — and then, remarkably, shipped a second open-source project (Agent Substrate) that schedules around Kubernetes, effectively admitting its own flagship platform has the wrong control plane for agent workloads. So what

Industry analysts now call the agent sandbox "the fourth compute primitive" after VMs, containers and serverless — and every cloud sells it first-party. Independent vendors have to win on multi-cloud neutrality, developer experience, iteration speed, and being the graduation path when a lab-managed agent product becomes too limiting.

01/Execution core · Code-execution sandboxes (pure-play)

Code-execution sandboxes.

The original category: companies whose whole business is selling disposable, isolated computers through an API. The technology itself has become a commodity — free, API-compatible clones now exist — so the value has moved to everything wrapped around it: network policy, secrets handling, snapshots, enterprise deployment, RL orchestration. As E2B's CEO put it: "A lot of new sandboxing companies focus on running sandboxes fast. That's not even half the story."

X/Head-to-head · where core beliefs collide

Twelve live arguments.

The places where named players hold directly opposing beliefs about how this market works. Each one: what the argument is about, what each side actually claims, and what evidence would settle it. These disagreements are where the next twelve months of product roadmaps are visible today.

  • 1 · Is a disposable "sandbox" even the right product — or do agents need a permanent computer? a16z's Martin Casado says the throwaway sandbox solves only a sliver of the problem: real work accumulates files, logins and running services, so agents need a full stateful machine ("AI agents just want a computer… this isn't yet another sandbox cloud"). The incumbents' answer is their revenue: Modal, E2B and Daytona — all disposable-first — took essentially all the growth. But watch their homepages: all three now use "computer" language and ship persistence features. The framing has already lost; the question is whether the products must eventually follow. It's settled if persistent, long-lived machines become the majority of incumbent revenue — then Casado won the argument even if his startup loses.
  • 2 · Are throwaway sandboxes obsolete? Fly.io's founder declared flatly: "The age of sandboxes is over." His logic: killing the computer after every task destroys the agent's accumulated context. The counter-evidence is enormous — one Daytona customer disposes of 850,000 sandboxes a day, and RL training is throwaway by nature. The honest resolution: the workloads split. Training and evaluation are structurally disposable; long-running office and coding agents accumulate state. OpenAI buying Ona specifically for persistent multi-day sandboxes is the strongest vote a lab has cast for the persistence side.
  • 3 · Is open source a security liability or the moat? (Same product, same quarter, opposite answers.) Daytona closed its code in June, arguing that publishing your isolation layer hands attackers "the exact blueprint… [for] an AI that never gets tired." E2B, one month later: "the only major open-source agentic sandbox platform… any smart business will want to own this infrastructure in-house." Developers sided loudly with E2B ("security by obscurity is not the answer") — but developers aren't the buyers, and Daytona's commercial growth predated its community anyway. This is the market's cleanest natural experiment. Watch for either a big regulated-enterprise deal won explicitly on auditability (E2B's bet), or a year of incident-free operation at Daytona (its bet).
  • 4 · Firecracker or gVisor — which isolation technology wins the enterprise? The microVM camp sells maximum isolation; AWS's VP put it bluntly: "I don't think anybody else has the level of security we do." The gVisor camp answers that its compatibility and scale advantages matter more — and the highest-valued vendor (Modal, $4.65B) plus Google both run gVisor. Complicating everything: Docker built a third option from scratch, and the Kubernetes standard made the choice a pluggable setting. Verdict so far: no dominant design, and "pluggable" is winning — which structurally favours orchestration vendors over primitive loyalists. (And ignore the "gVisor escaped 18% of AISI runs" stat if you see it in a deck — it's not in the actual paper.)
  • 5 · Should agent infrastructure extend Kubernetes or route around it? Daytona's CEO gave a conference talk literally titled "Kubernetes Is Not Your Sandbox" — the argument being that Kubernetes' control plane is too slow for millions of short-lived, bursty agent machines. The awkward supporting witness is Google itself: it shipped a Kubernetes-native agent sandbox and a second project that bypasses Kubernetes' scheduler, hedging both sides. Physics favours the bypass camp (every performance leader runs a custom scheduler); enterprise distribution favours Kubernetes. Watch whether AWS or Azure adopt Google's bypass project — that would create a post-Kubernetes standard.
  • 6 · Is browser infrastructure a standalone company, a platform feature, or an open-source commodity? Browserbase's founding thesis was that the unpleasant parts of web automation are a durable moat because labs won't touch them. Since then: Cloudflare built an agent browser from scratch (Kitesurf), the open-source Browser Use started selling browser-hours at $0.02 (a fraction of Browserbase's price) on its own forked infrastructure, and Anthropic handed its browser slot to Kernel. Klein publicly considering renaming his company away from "browser" is the tell that even the leader thinks pure browser infra isn't the terminal position. Settled by: Kitesurf's price at general availability, and the terms of Browserbase's next round.
  • 7 · RL environments: three-to-five winners, sixty-vendor gold rush, or absorbed by the labs? Wing VC predicts decisive consolidation into 3–5 leaders by 2030, like data labeling before it. The trackers still count 35–60 active vendors. The events so far support Wing's endpoint but through a faster mechanism — absorption: Mercor bought Deeptune, Google is reportedly license-and-hiring Mechanize. Against full lab-insourcing: OpenAI's own fine-tuning service reportedly underperforms, with demand flowing to startups at a fifth of the cost. The scarce proof either way is revenue from customers who aren't labs — Prime Intellect's 6,000-customer base is the best evidence it exists.
  • 8 · "Execution is commoditizing" versus a 24x funding boom — both can't be right? They can, split by layer. The bare primitive (fast, isolated VM) genuinely is commoditized — Tencent gives an API-compatible clone away free. The $504M of venture money is buying everything wrapped around the primitive: network policy, secrets brokering, snapshots, RL orchestration, enterprise deployment. As E2B's CEO put it: "running sandboxes fast — that's not even half the story." The investable question for any sandbox deal is exactly which wrapped layer it owns.
  • 9 · Which operating system becomes the agent's desktop? Linux wins headless server fleets by default. The case for Windows is where the money is: enterprise back-office work lives in Windows applications, Daytona shipped licensed Windows sandboxes, OpenAI built a custom Windows sandbox for Codex, and Microsoft launched a whole Windows 365 for Agents program. macOS is legally capped (two VMs per machine under Apple's license) yet Apple's container project is one of the fastest-growing repos in the space — for local, on-device agents rather than cloud fleets. Not either/or: three fronts with different winners.
  • 10 · Do GitHub stars still mean anything here? The decoupling is now extreme in both directions: E2B's stars are flat while its business booms; Daytona's stars went negative while it grows 74% a month; Vercel's sandbox SDK has 183 stars and 100+ releases. Meanwhile Browser Use and Tencent's CubeSandbox rode stars into genuinely real positions. The refined read: stars now measure distribution and standards adoption (whose API becomes the compatibility target — that's where E2B's open source pays), but they no longer predict revenue, and treating them as a moat signal will mislead you in this category.
  • 11 · Are sandbox escapes an existential threat or security theater? On the facts, theater: every 2026 incident traced to a configuration the sandbox was told to allow, and the UK's systematic benchmark found zero novel escapes. But the fear is commercially real and productive — it just buys the wrong product if it buys thicker VM walls. The bug class that actually recurs is authorization (CoreBreak), and the products that fix it are egress proxies and credential brokers. One confirmed cross-tenant breakout at a major provider would flip this argument overnight and re-price the whole category.
  • 12 · "Labs won't own the icky parts" — does the thesis survive? In its broad form, no: OpenAI bought a sandbox company, AWS sells sandboxes directly, Cloudflare built the browser, Docker entered. It survives in exactly one redoubt — no lab will ever ship CAPTCHA-solving or stealth browsing under its own brand, so that sliver of Browserbase's original thesis holds. The successor question is subtler: labs now run "approved provider" programs where vendors compete for slots. Is a slot durable distribution, or the first step to being commoditized by whoever owns the slot? Watch whether Anthropic or Cognition ever drop their external providers after building enough in-house.
The meta-pattern across all twelve: the incumbents keep winning the revenue while conceding the argument — E2B adopted its critics' "computer" vocabulary, Daytona abandoned the open-source identity it championed six months earlier, Google built the Kubernetes bypass it publicly didn't need, and Browserbase's CEO is contemplating dropping "browser" from the name. In a market this young, vocabulary migrations predict product roadmaps about a year ahead of the funding announcements.

★/Who wins each layer · the scorecard

Who wins each category.

The partner-meeting answer for each layer, with the reasoning attached.

  • Code-execution sandboxes: Modal is winning the money (scale + platform bundling), E2B the ecosystem (its API became the standard everyone clones), Daytona the feature race (Windows, GPU, RL). Because the raw primitive is free now, back only companies whose wedge compounds — an API standard plus enterprise deployment, or a hard-to-replicate capability like licensed Windows. Anything whose pitch is "we're fast" is competing with a free Tencent download.
  • Hyperscaler runtimes: AWS is executing best; Google is thinking hardest (its Kubernetes-bypass project is the most interesting architecture in the layer). The giants will own the compliance-default segment where "use what's already in our cloud" wins by inertia. The startups' durable edges: multi-cloud neutrality, faster iteration, and being the graduation path when a lab-managed agent product becomes too constraining.
  • Browser infrastructure: still a real category, but the generic middle is getting crushed. Kernel has the best wedge-per-dollar (identity plus the Anthropic anchor); Browser Use owns the price floor. Browserbase's next round is the referendum on whether the category's premium tier persists.
  • Computer-use: a feature, not a category — with two exceptions worth backing: Windows desktops (licensing economics plus lab benchmark demand create real scarcity) and identity-heavy desktop automation in regulated industries. Cua is the asymmetric bet: category-defining mindshare on half a million dollars.
  • RL environments: a barbell. Either own the open standard plus broad enterprise distribution (Prime Intellect's position), or own deep bespoke trust with one or two labs and price accordingly (Mechanize — accepting that the likely exit structure favours founders over investors). Avoid the middle: environment-content production without distribution is what Mercor and Scale are absorbing.
  • Snapshot/branching: genuinely differentiated technology everywhere, but a features-not-companies risk — the capabilities keep getting absorbed into bigger platforms. Namespace is the interesting one because its position (the compute layer under other companies' agent products) compounds if it diversifies its dependents.
  • Security & credential brokering: the best new-wedge risk/reward on this map. It fixes the failure mode that actually recurs (authorization, not isolation), developers love it, hyperscalers just demonstrated they get it wrong too, and Cisco×Astrix proved the exit path.
  • Durable orchestration: Temporal's win is confirmed and priced. The middle of the layer faces free platform bundles; the independents there need to become the multi-cloud default quickly or become features.
