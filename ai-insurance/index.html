T/The thesis · 60 seconds

Supply is running well ahead of demand.

Total bound AI-liability premium worldwide is best estimated in the tens of millions of dollars. Three major carriers — AIG, Great American, W.R. Berkley — went to regulators asking permission to exclude AI liability. Coverage is being withdrawn faster and more credibly than the new entrants are writing it.

Where value is being made
Operations software with auditable demand — mea Platform ($400bn+ GWP processed, Lloyd's and AXIS as customers) and FurtherAI ($20bn TIV in 3 months). The capital layer: Accelerant earns fees on $4.3bn of premium while reinsuring 92% of its own away. And claims cost — Lemonade's 5% LAE.
Where it isn't
The AI-native carrier cohort has not broadly beaten incumbents: Root 98.2% combined (worse YoY), Hippo 99% — Progressive 90.7%. Europe has less AI liability law than a year ago. Standalone AI assurance is being absorbed, not built — four exits in 18 months.
Open questions worth a check
Who actually carries the risk — AIUC's Lloyd's syndicate is named nowhere. Does enterprise procurement start demanding AI cover the way it demanded SOC 2? And the empty niches: agent transaction bonding, GPU parametric downtime, regulated-profession AI cover.
  • The market is datable, and that's unusual. Everything in the insurance-for-AI cluster is downstream of three ISO endorsements — CG 40 47, CG 40 48, CG 35 08 — taking effect on 1 January 2026.
  • Warranty beats liability on insurability, and always has. Munich Re's aiSure has run since 2018 because a benchmark miss is measurable and bounded. Third-party AI liability is neither. The 2026 cohort chose the harder problem.
  • Value accrues to flow, not risk. Accelerant reinsures 92% of its premium away. mea Platform processes $400bn of GWP and touches none of it. The best businesses here refuse the risk.
  • AI's proven edge is expense, not loss. Lemonade's record 5% LAE ratio against an industry ~9% is the strongest number in this map. Cost-to-serve, not risk selection.
  • The incumbents are winning the software layer. Guidewire, Duck Creek, Verisk, Earnix and Moody's have bought eight of the most interesting companies here. Not one AI-native challenger has been acquired.
  • Capital is concentrating, not broadening. A record quarter with a narrowing pipeline: deals ≥$100M took 68.4% of Q2'26, early-stage fell 51.8%, and insurer participation dropped to 27 deals.
  • Nobody has a claim yet. ~140–150 active US AI lawsuits, but zero confirmed AI-liability claim payments and zero AI-exclusion coverage disputes in court. The first denied claim that reaches a judge reprices this entire market.
  • Autonomy claims need discounting by default. Two of the three most impressive things we were told did not survive verification — while Ki has quietly bound risk algorithmically since 2020 and newer entrants claim the frontier.
  • CEE is nearly absent. ~15–20 insurtechs across the whole region against 194 in the UK alone. Ominimo is the one real win — and it's an MGA, not a carrier.
★/The structural fact · exclusions vs affirmative cover

Three endorsements created this market.

Stated precisely, because secondary coverage mangles it. These are optional, carrier-elected CGL endorsements, effective 1 January 2026. The trigger is a defined "trained generative model" — so traditional and predictive ML arguably falls outside ISO's scope, though not outside broader carrier-specific forms.

FormScope
CG 40 47Broad — excludes bodily injury, property damage and personal/advertising injury "arising out of, or attributable to, generative artificial intelligence"
CG 40 48Narrow — personal & advertising injury only
CG 35 08Products / Completed Operations part — bodily injury and property damage only
The number everyone gets wrong. The widely-quoted ~82% is ISO's standing as the standard-forms provider across the whole US P&C market, all lines, unrelated to AI. It is the addressable base. It does not mean 82% of policies now exclude AI. Actual attachment is unmeasured — a Verisk executive told Carrier Management (23 Jul 2026) he "didn't know how many insurers are adopting them yet."

Who is excluding

  • W.R. Berkley — first mover, an "absolute" AI exclusion reaching D&O/E&O/fiduciary and capturing traditional AI, not just generative.
  • AIG — filed, but told regulators it has no immediate plan to invoke. It wants the option.
  • Berkshire Hathaway · Travelers · Great American — confirmed filings.
  • Chubb — narrower than reported elsewhere: systemic and correlated AI events only, not AI harms generally.
  • Cincinnati Financial · Philadelphia · Frederick Mutual · Hamilton Select — confirmed adopters.

Who is going affirmative

  • CFC — the most substantial move, and one we initially got wrong: affirmative AI wording embedded across seven products from ~24–26 June 2026.
  • Coalition — AI Endorsement (2024) plus a Deepfake Response Endorsement (Dec 2025). Embedded and default-on, which is probably how this risk actually scales.
  • AXA XL — GenAI cyber endorsement.
  • QBE — a live LLM-jacking sub-limit: ~$250K on a $5M cyber policy. That is 5%, not the 10% figure in circulation, per QBE's own worked example.
  • Beazley — not affirmative. Discussing amended wording, publicly denying any retreat, and the amendments were not applied to active policies as of April 2026.

02/Insurance for AI · Model performance warranties

Model performance warranties.

The older, quieter, more insurable idea: don't cover third-party liability — guarantee the model performs to a stated benchmark. "Every AI system is just a probabilistic system… there will always be a probability that the AI or GenAI makes mistakes or hallucinates. This cannot be technically avoided." — Michael Berger, Head of Insure AI, Munich Re

CompanyStructureWhy it matters
Munich Re aiSure™
PUBLIC
 Live since 2018 · reinsurer balance sheet · up to $15M per risk The originator, seven years ahead of the 2026 rush, and still the leader. Guarantees model performance against agreed metrics rather than insuring downstream harm. A German reinsurer founded in 1880 owns this category — no startup, anywhere. Note it is first-party performance cover, not third-party liability — a distinction repeatedly blurred in market-sizing writeups.
Armilla Guaranteed
 Launched Oct 2023 · backed by Swiss Re, Greenlight Re, Chaucer The only startup running warranty and liability side by side — the best natural experiment available on which structure buyers actually want.
The distinction practitioners keep drawing. A warranty pays when the model misses a benchmark agreed in advance — measurable trigger, bounded loss. Liability pays when the model causes loss to a third party — neither measurable nor bounded. That is why the warranty layer has run profitably since 2018 while the liability layer is eight months old.

?/Demand · does anyone actually buy this

Governance demand is ahead of insurance demand.

The sharpest finding in the whole map, and it cuts against the bulls: ISO 42001 governance certification is already appearing in third-party-risk questionnaires. AIUC-1 — the insurance-linked standard — is explicitly not yet on most of them. Enterprises are demanding AI governance as table stakes. They are not yet demanding AI insurance. The bull case requires that second step, and it hasn't happened.

Total bound premium

Tens of $M
Globally. Not billions. Do not use the $6.8bn figure in circulation — it comes from a low-credibility vendor report, and a specialty broker actively selling into this market states unprompted that no reliable public figure exists.

Confirmed buyers

1
ElevenLabs, Feb 2026, 3M+ voice agents, via AIUC-1. Independently confirmed — and still the only fully-verified transaction of its kind. One datapoint, not a pattern.

Carriers asking to exit

3
AIG, Great American and W.R. Berkley went to US regulators asking permission to exclude AI liability. For a supply-side bet, that is more decision-relevant than any survey.

Evidence for demand

  • Geneva Association survey — methodology verified. 600 corporate decision-makers, 100 each across six markets, online, Feb 2025, 22 questions. >90% interested; ~two-thirds would pay 10%+ more premium.
  • But weight it carefully. It measures stated intent; the authors themselves flag adverse selection (demand concentrates among firms that already had severe AI failures); and respondents split roughly evenly on which product should even cover this — cyber, standalone, property/BI, or a liability extension. The market hasn't converged on a category.
  • Rising claims pressure in professional lines. More than half of 13 major legal-malpractice insurers — covering ~80% of the AmLaw 200 — report rising AI claims, against 495 hallucination-in-filing incidents since 2023, 222 in 2026 alone.

Evidence against

  • Frontier-lab indemnities are free competing cover — but only for IP. OpenAI, Anthropic, Google and Databricks all indemnify, scoped to IP infringement only. So the free indemnity suppresses demand for the easiest risk to price, leaving hallucination and agentic-action harm as the only thing worth a standalone product — precisely the class carriers most want to exclude.
  • Shostack's bear case. "No customer will ever see a payout. We can see the dynamic that's emerged in cybersecurity and learn from it." It rests on the cyber analogy — mispriced premiums, attribution problems, misrepresentation exclusions — plus a structural claim that hallucination is uncontrollable by design. Fair counter: courts have a real doctrine against exclusions that "swallow" the coverage grant, so "never" is a prediction, not settled law.
  • No claims experience at all. ~140–150 active US AI lawsuits is confirmed, but zero confirmed AI-liability claim payments and zero AI-exclusion coverage disputes in litigation. Nippon Life v. OpenAI (4 Mar 2026) is a corporate plaintiff suing OpenAI directly, not a coverage case. The nH Predict litigation is the inverse risk — insurers using AI to deny claims — and should not be conflated.
Where compelled demand might actually start: professional indemnity. Malpractice and E&O are already conditions of licensure in medicine, law and audit — unlike general commercial AI liability. Nothing is compelled today: state human-in-the-loop laws (Arizona, Indiana, Utah, Maryland, all 2026) mandate review and disclosure processes, not insurance purchase. But PI insurers are already underwriting AI exposure into renewals, and insurers themselves flag "silent AI" in PI books as unresolved. That is the most plausible front door.

The regulatory verdict — and it inverts the common assumption

Europe has narrowed its own AI liability exposure rather than creating insurable demand.

  • The AI Liability Directive was withdrawn. Announced in the Commission's 2025 Work Programme (11 Feb 2025), formally withdrawn per OJ notice C/2025/5423 (6 Oct 2025).
  • Its replacement excludes the main B2B harm. The revised Product Liability Directive (2024/2853) applies to products placed on market after 9 Dec 2026 and does cover AI and software — but Recital 24 explicitly excludes pure economic loss, privacy infringement and discrimination from strict liability. That is exactly the scenario where an AI error causes a counterparty financial loss.
  • AI Act high-risk obligations were delayed. Via the Digital Omnibus, now law (Reg. 2026/1744, in force 27 Jul 2026): standalone high-risk to 2 Dec 2027, embedded high-risk to 2 Aug 2028. Insurance pricing for life and health is explicitly named in Annex III. Transparency and deepfake rules were not delayed and are live from 2 Aug 2026.
  • EIOPA is lobbying in the other direction. Its Aug 2025 Opinion is interpretive, not new rules — and a Mar 2026 board letter seeks to exclude GLMs and GAMs, i.e. most traditional actuarial pricing, from high-risk classification entirely.
  • The UK explicitly refuses a bespoke AI regime. PRA and FCA rely on SM&CR and Consumer Duty. Lloyd's lobbies for the status quo and frames AI as a sales opportunity.
  • Net: the insurable gap persists because regulation stepped back from filling it. What capacity exists is market-led and follows the US pattern — it is not EU-regulation-driven.

Where Europe has nobody

  • AI-native full-stack carrier for startups (the Corgi/Nirvana model) — zero European equivalent. Ominimo is deliberately MGA-only.
  • AI underwriting-decision SaaS at scale (Sixfold/Federato model) — hyperexponential and Cytora exist, but nothing at Sixfold's scale and no CEE player at all.
  • Standalone AI-liability or performance-warranty insurtech — this layer is owned by 145-year-old Munich Re, not by any startup anywhere in Europe. The most open lane on this list.
  • A CEE-origin AI-for-insurance software company of real scale — does not exist.
  • A CEE-founded reinsurance-tech or capacity platform — Supercede is closest, and it is UK-domiciled.

Easy to misclassify.

Included because this map was built during an active investment process. Three unrelated companies share the name — riskcube.com (San Francisco, this one), riskcube.insure (Mumbai, an IRDAI-licensed composite broker doing reinsurance placement and cat modelling — the one that actually matches an "AI reinsurance" framing), and riskcube.ae (a UAE risk-advisory consultancy). No European or CEE RiskCube exists.

What it is

  • A licensed brokerage. One intake form across 40+ AM Best-rated carriers, licensed-broker review, 24-hour COI. Lines: D&O, Tech E&O, Cyber, GL.
  • $500K raised in total, nothing since. YC F24. Headcount 1–3 (LinkedIn and Crunchbase say 1; YC's own profile says 3).
  • Solo founder Andrei Craciunescu — ex-WTW Risk & Analytics ~9 years, MSc maths LMU Munich, PhD research TU Munich. German, not CEE — Munich-raised and educated, self-describes as "originally from Munich."
  • Licensing is clean. California #6017028 and Delaware #3004000392, effective 11 Feb 2026. An apparent June/July timeline conflict is stale marketing copy, not a real inconsistency.

What it is not

  • Not a carrier, MGA, reinsurer or underwriting-technology company. No balance-sheet risk, no proprietary underwriting data.
  • It does not compete with Cytora, Supercede, Federato, Kalepa or CyberCube — those sell analytics to carriers and reinsurers, on the opposite side of the value chain.
  • Its comparables are Vouch, Corgi, Coverdash, Embroker and Newfront.
  • Not exposure to the AI-liability underwriting opportunity this map describes. That sits in layer 01 and the capacity table.
The claim that could not be verified. "Exclusive AI capacity" — no carrier, MGA or Lloyd's syndicate is named anywhere public. This is the central differentiating claim and it is unsupported by public evidence. For contrast: Armilla names Chaucer, Testudo names Apollo/Atrium/QBE, Mayflower names Hadron and Aon. Naming your capacity is the norm among credible entrants. Also unverifiable: any named customer, GWP, premium placed or revenue — all public material is anecdotal, including a demo logo ("Acme AI"). State footprint is two states against a "nationwide" positioning.

Questions to put to the company

  • Who provides the "exclusive AI capacity" — which carrier or syndicate, on what paper, what limits and aggregate, and is it a binding authority or case-by-case placement?
  • Given the ISO exclusions took effect 1 Jan 2026, what exactly does the excess umbrella layer cover that the underlying policy now excludes — and has any carrier confirmed in writing that it will pay on an AI-caused loss?
  • Actual headcount, licensed producers, and full state footprint against the "nationwide" claim.
  • GWP placed, customer count, revenue, retention, CAC. None of this is public for anyone in this layer, so it has to come from the company.
  • Which "partnerships" are exclusive rather than discount codes? Vouch and Corgi both run active YC-discount pages competing for the same channel RiskCube came through.
  • What is the intended path — stay a broker, become an MGA, or become a risk-bearer? Vouch spent $200M+ and six years reaching carrier status then sold it to Hiscox; Corgi took the RRG route because it skips the rated-carrier capital regime; Anzen died in the middle.
Framing for the investment committee. This is a pre-seed distribution bet on a single technical founder in a segment with credible, vastly better-capitalised competitors — not a technology bet, not a data-moat bet, and not exposure to AI risk transfer. If the thesis is "own a piece of AI risk transfer," this doesn't provide it. If the thesis is "the fastest, best-distributed broker for AI-native companies takes the segment before incumbents notice," that is legitimate — but it rests entirely on distribution exclusivity and founder execution, and the capacity claim needs substantiating before it counts as a differentiator at all. The layer's own history is not encouraging for pure brokers: Vouch reversed, Anzen died, Embroker has been silent five years, Founder Shield became a subsidiary, and Newfront exited at a 41% discount to its peak.
★/Cross-cutting themes

Nine threads through the stack.

Patterns that recur across every layer — the ideas the rest of this map is downstream of.

  • The market has a birthday, and that is unusual. Everything in the insurance-for-AI cluster is downstream of three ISO endorsements taking effect on 1 January 2026 — which makes this market unusually datable, and unusually dependent on a single regulatory-adjacent event holding.
  • Coverage is being withdrawn more credibly than it is being written. Exclusions are documented in primary sources and adopted by Berkshire, Travelers, Chubb and W.R. Berkley. Affirmative cover is mostly embedded endorsements from Coalition, CFC and AXA XL. The gap is real; the filling of it is not yet.
  • Warranty beats liability on insurability, and always has. aiSure has run since 2018 because a benchmark miss is measurable and bounded. Third-party AI liability is neither. The 2026 cohort chose the harder problem.
  • Value accrues to flow, not risk. Accelerant reinsures 92% of its premium away and earns fees on $4.3bn. mea Platform processes $400bn of GWP and touches none of it. The best businesses here refuse the risk.
  • AI's proven edge is expense, not loss. Lemonade's 5% LAE against ~9% industry is the strongest number in this map. Root at 98.2% and Hippo at 99% against Progressive's 90.7% is the corrective. Cost-to-serve, not risk selection.
  • The incumbents are winning the software layer. Guidewire, Duck Creek, Verisk, Earnix and Moody's have bought eight of the most interesting companies here. Not one AI-native challenger has been acquired — read that as incumbents buying what they can absorb, not as challengers winning.
  • Capital is concentrating, not broadening. Q2 2026 hit $2.44bn across 107 deals with 99.1% to AI-labelled startups — while early-stage funding fell 51.8% QoQ ($548.5M → $264.2M), deals ≥$100M took 68.4%, and insurer participation dropped to 27 deals. A record quarter with a narrowing pipeline, driven by VC/PE rather than insurers.
  • Nobody has a claim yet. No AI-liability claim payment and no AI-exclusion coverage dispute has surfaced. Every price in the insurance-for-AI cluster is set on litigation-trend proxies. The first denied claim that reaches court reprices the entire market.
  • Autonomy claims need discounting by default. Two of the three most impressive things this research was told did not survive verification — while Ki has quietly bound risk algorithmically since 2020 and newer entrants claim the frontier.
